Why Did My Home Insurance Go Up in Florida? 2026 Premium Guide

Homeowners Insurance March 30, 2026 16 min read

You opened the renewal letter, saw the number, and said something you probably shouldn't repeat in front of your kids. You're not alone. Florida's average annual cost of home insurance hit $8,292 in 2025 — an 18% spike over the prior year, according to Insurify's 2026 Insuring the American Homeowner Report. That's the steepest single-year jump in a decade, and for a lot of Palm Beach County homeowners, the number on the renewal notice still landed like a gut punch even after years of steady increases.

But here's the thing most guides won't tell you: the story in 2026 is actually more complicated than "everything went up." Some carriers filed rate decreases for the first time in years. Others are still pushing double-digit hikes. Whether your premium went up, down, or sideways depends on your specific carrier, your roof, your location, your coverage limits, and a handful of factors you can actually control.

This is a step-by-step tutorial. We'll decode exactly why your premium changed, figure out which drivers hit your policy hardest, and walk through every lever you can pull to bring that number down. No fluff, no vague advice to "call your agent." Actual steps.

What's Actually Happening to Florida Premiums in 2026

The headline number is attention-grabbing, but you need the full picture. Florida remains the most expensive state in the country for homeowners insurance. For a standardized policy with $300,000 in dwelling coverage, Florida homeowners pay an average of $5,688 per year — roughly 199% higher than the national average, according to Insurify (2026).

But the rate of increase is finally decelerating. Insurify projects premiums will rise another 2% by the end of 2026, which is a dramatic slowdown from the 18% jump in 2025 and the double-digit annual increases that preceded it.

More importantly, the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation (OIR) reported a landmark shift: as of November 2025, regulators had received 73 filings for rate decreases and 94 filings for 0% increases, according to the Florida OIR (2025). That's a market turning point. Several major carriers have already received approval for cuts that will flow through on renewals throughout 2026.

Step 1: Decode Your Renewal Notice

Before you do anything else, pull out your actual renewal declaration page. You need four numbers:

  1. Prior premium — what you paid last year
  2. New premium — what they're asking for this year
  3. Dwelling coverage (Coverage A) — the maximum your insurer will pay to rebuild your home
  4. Deductible(s) — your all-peril deductible and, separately, your hurricane/windstorm deductible (these are different in Florida — see our hurricane deductible guide for the full breakdown)

Now do the math: (New Premium − Prior Premium) ÷ Prior Premium × 100 = Your Percentage Change. Write that number down. You'll reference it through every step below.

What to Look for on the Dec Page

Your declaration page also lists every endorsement and coverage sublimit on your policy. Check for these common premium inflaters:

  • Water damage sublimit or exclusion added — Some carriers have started sublimiting water damage to $10,000 or excluding it entirely. If your carrier removed or restricted water coverage, your premium might look lower but your actual protection shrank. If a claim under your current policy gets denied — including through an automated review process — Florida's HB 527 AI claims denial protections give you specific rights to challenge it.
  • Roof coverage changed from Replacement Cost to Actual Cash Value — This is the single most common stealth downgrade in Florida renewals. If your roof is over 10 years old, check whether your carrier shifted you to ACV (depreciated value). Our roof age insurance law guide explains the HB 815 protections you should know about.
  • Ordinance or Law coverage reduced — If your home was built before current building codes (most Palm Beach County homes were — median build year is 1984, per Redfin), you need Ordinance or Law coverage to pay for code-required upgrades after a loss.

Your premium isn't one number — it's a stack of risk factors multiplied together. Here's what's pushing each layer up or down in 2026.

If your premium went up despite your carrier filing for a decrease, one of these individual factors likely overshadowed the rate-level reduction. The most common culprits: your home's replacement cost was reassessed upward, or your roof crossed an age threshold.

Reinsurance: The Hidden Cost That's Finally Dropping

Reinsurance is the insurance that insurance companies buy to protect themselves against catastrophic losses. For years, Florida's reinsurance costs were among the highest in the world — and those costs got passed directly to you.

Good news: Aon's Head of Florida Strategy reported reinsurance rate decreases of 10% to 15% for the January 1, 2026 renewals, according to a Florida Chamber panel reported by Reinsurance News (2025). If the 2026 hurricane season passes without major losses, reinsurance pricing could decline another 10% at the next renewal cycle.

This is the single biggest reason carriers are able to file rate decreases for the first time in years.

Replacement Cost: The Factor That Keeps Climbing

Even when your carrier's base rate drops, your premium can still go up if the estimated cost to rebuild your home increased. And in Florida, rebuild costs keep climbing.

Building a home in Florida runs $150 to $350 per square foot depending on location and material quality, according to Seanote Construction (2025). The national average reconstruction cost rose 4.1% in 2024 alone, per Verisk's reconstruction cost analysis. But two factors are making Florida worse than the national average:

  • Tariffs on building materials — As of 2025, there's a 25% tariff on Mexican goods, a 35% tariff on Canadian lumber, and a 50% tariff on steel, aluminum, and copper imports, per federal trade data (2025). Southeast U.S. wood prices alone jumped 8.2% between June 2024 and June 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
  • Hurricane building code compliance — Florida's strict wind resistance codes (a good thing for safety) make reconstruction significantly more expensive than in states with weaker codes.

The average replacement cost of U.S. homes rose 55% between 2020 and 2023, according to Worth Insurance (2025). If your insurer updated your Coverage A to reflect current rebuild costs, that alone could explain a 5–15% premium increase even with a flat or declining rate.

Step 3: Check Whether Your Carrier Filed for an Increase or Decrease

This is the step most homeowners skip, and it's the most important one. The Florida OIR maintains a public database called IRFS (Insurance Regulation Filing Search) at floir.com where you can look up your carrier's most recent rate filing.

Here's how:

  1. Go to the OIR IRFS search page
  2. Enter your insurance company's name
  3. Filter by form type: "Rate"
  4. Look at the most recent filing and check the overall rate impact percentage

If your carrier filed for a decrease but your premium went up, the increase is coming from your individual risk profile — not the carrier's rates. That tells you to focus on Steps 5 through 7 below.

If your carrier filed for an increase, the table in the first section of this guide shows you exactly where the major carriers stand. You can also use the OIR's CHOICES rate comparison tool to compare what other carriers would charge for your specific property.

Step 4: Understand the Litigation Reform Effect

Florida's insurance market didn't break overnight, and it won't heal overnight either. But the legislative reforms under SB 2-A (2022) are delivering measurable results that are finally reaching your renewal notice.

Here's the short version: Florida used to account for a wildly disproportionate share of homeowners insurance lawsuits nationally. SB 2-A eliminated one-way attorney's fees (where insurers paid plaintiff attorneys even in partial settlements), banned assignment-of-benefits abuse, and allowed carriers to include mandatory binding arbitration clauses. For the full legislative breakdown, see our Florida insurance crisis explainer.

The results, three years in: every month of 2025 reported a continued decline in litigation compared to the same month the previous year, according to the Governor's office (2026). That legal environment stabilization is the structural reason carriers can file decreases — it's not charity, it's math. Lower lawsuit costs = lower loss projections = lower rates.

Additionally, 17 new insurers have entered the Florida market since the reforms took effect, according to the Florida OIR (2025). More carriers competing for your business means more pressure to offer competitive pricing.

Step 5: Run a Policy Self-Audit

Pull out your dec page again. We're going line by line.

Coverage A (Dwelling)

Is your dwelling coverage within 10% of your home's actual replacement cost? Not market value — replacement cost. A $500,000 Palm Beach County home might cost $375,000 to $875,000 to rebuild depending on size and finishes, per Seanote Construction (2025). If your Coverage A is significantly above your true rebuild cost, you're overpaying. If it's below, you're underinsured and at risk of a coinsurance penalty after a loss.

Hurricane Deductible

Florida allows percentage-based hurricane deductibles of 2%, 5%, or 10% of your dwelling coverage. On a home insured for $480,000 (close to the Zillow median for Palm Beach County), a 2% deductible means $9,600 out of pocket versus $48,000 at 10%. Higher deductibles lower your premium, but make sure you can actually float that amount after a storm. Our hurricane deductible guide walks through the math for every scenario.

Flood Insurance

Homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage. Period. If you don't have a separate flood policy, a single flooding event could wipe you out — and 35% of Palm Beach County properties sit in flood zones. Check whether you need NFIP or private flood coverage.

Wind Mitigation Credits

If you have a current wind mitigation inspection report, check that every qualifying feature is reflected in your policy. Missing credits are free money left on the table. If you don't have a wind mitigation report, get one — through the My Safe Florida Home program it's free, and the inspection alone can unlock significant premium reductions through Florida Statute 627.0629 credits. Use our hurricane season insurance checklist to make sure your coverage is locked in before June 1.

Step 6: Shop the Market

This is where the real money is. Florida homeowners who comparison shop save between 10% and 20% on their premiums, according to Insurify (2026). Homeowners who shop annually save $200 to $500 compared to simply auto-renewing, per Insurance.com (2025).

The rate variation between carriers is staggering. For the same home and coverage profile, quotes can range from around $3,700 per year to over $20,000 per year depending on the carrier, according to MoneyGeek (2026). That's not a rounding error — that's the difference between manageable and unaffordable.

How to Shop Effectively

  1. Start 60 days before renewal. Carriers need time to quote, and you need time to compare. Don't wait for the renewal notice — initiate the process proactively.

  2. Use the OIR CHOICES tool. The Florida Office of Insurance Regulation provides a free rate comparison search tool that shows estimated premiums from multiple carriers for your specific property characteristics.

  3. Get at least three quotes. From different carrier types — one large national (State Farm, Allstate), one Florida-focused carrier (Heritage, Security First, Florida Peninsula), and one newer entrant. The 17 new carriers that entered Florida's market post-reform are often pricing aggressively to build book.

  4. Compare apples to apples. Match dwelling coverage, deductible levels, and liability limits across quotes. A $500 cheaper quote with a 10% hurricane deductible instead of 2% isn't actually cheaper — it's a different product.

  5. Ask about new-business credits. Some carriers offer first-year discounts of 5–10% to attract policyholders switching from competitors. These disappear at renewal, so factor that in, but they're real savings in year one.

Step 7: Stack Every Available Discount

Once you've chosen a carrier, make sure you're capturing every discount you qualify for. These compound.

Bundling (15–25% savings): Combining home and auto with the same carrier consistently delivers the largest single discount, according to Insurify (2026). If your auto is with a different carrier, run the numbers on bundling.

Wind mitigation (up to 40% savings): Impact windows, hurricane shutters, reinforced roof-to-wall connections, secondary water resistance — each feature triggers a specific credit under Florida's wind mitigation framework. A full suite of hardening features can cut your wind premium nearly in half. The My Safe Florida Home program provides free inspections and grants to help cover the cost.

Security and safety devices (5–15% savings): Monitored alarm systems, fire/smoke detection, and water leak sensors each qualify for modest but stackable credits depending on your carrier.

Claims-free history (5–20% savings): Most carriers reward three or more consecutive claim-free years. If you filed a small claim recently, do the math on whether the payout was worth the premium impact.

Roof replacement: If your roof is over 15 years old and you can afford to replace it, the premium reduction from a new roof plus the wind mitigation credits on modern roofing systems often pays back the investment within 5–8 years. Our roof age law guide covers the HB 815 protections against age-based nonrenewals and the insurance math on replacement.

The Bigger Picture: Market Recovery Is Real but Uneven

Here's the honest summary. Florida's insurance market is stabilizing. The reform legislation is working. Litigation is down. New carriers are entering. Reinsurance costs are falling. Citizens policyholders are down to 395,144 — a 50% reduction from the prior year and the lowest level in 14 years, according to Citizens Property Insurance (2025). Policies are moving back to the private market at the fastest pace in a decade.

But "stabilizing" doesn't mean "cheap." Florida still has the most expensive homeowners insurance in the country by a wide margin. Construction costs keep climbing. Climate risk hasn't gone anywhere. And the carriers that lost money for years aren't going to slash rates to pre-crisis levels anytime soon.

The homeowners who fare best in this environment are the ones who treat their insurance like any other major annual purchase: they audit their coverage, shop the market, stack discounts, and make strategic decisions about deductibles and hardening investments. If you haven't done that in the last 12 months, start now — spring renewal season in Palm Beach County is the right time.

For a deeper look at how the crisis developed and the full reform timeline, read our Florida homeowners insurance crisis explainer. And if you're a Citizens policyholder wondering about the clearinghouse transition to private carriers, our SB 1028 guide breaks down the opt-out rules and timeline.

And don't forget: hurricane season starts June 1. Any coverage changes should be locked in before then.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my Florida home insurance go up even though my carrier filed for a rate decrease?

Rate filings set the overall average change across a carrier's book of business. Your individual premium depends on your specific risk profile — roof age, ZIP code, claims history, replacement cost, and credit-based insurance score. If your home's replacement cost was reassessed upward or your roof crossed an age threshold, those factors can push your premium up even when the carrier's average rate went down, per Florida OIR filing guidelines (2025).

How much can I save by shopping for home insurance in Florida?

Florida homeowners who comparison shop save 10% to 20% on premiums, according to industry data. Given Florida's average premium of $8,292 (Insurify, 2025), that translates to potential savings of $830 to $1,660 annually. The Florida OIR's free CHOICES rate comparison tool at choices.floir.gov lets you compare carrier estimates for your specific property.

Is Florida home insurance going to keep going up in 2026?

The rate of increase is slowing dramatically. After an 18% jump in 2025, Insurify projects a further 2% increase by the end of 2026 — essentially flat by recent standards. Multiple carriers have filed for decreases, and reinsurance costs fell 10–15% for the January 2026 renewal cycle (Reinsurance News, 2025). A quiet hurricane season could drive further relief in 2027.

What is the average cost of homeowners insurance in Florida in 2026?

Florida's average annual home insurance cost reached $8,292 in 2025, per Insurify's 2026 report. For a standardized policy with $300,000 in dwelling coverage and a $1,000 deductible, the average is $5,688, making Florida the most expensive state for homeowners insurance. Actual costs in Palm Beach County vary widely based on proximity to the coast, roof age, and construction type.

How do I check my insurance carrier's rate filing in Florida?

Visit the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation's IRFS (Insurance Regulation Filing Search) at irfssearch.floir.gov. Enter your carrier's name, filter by "Rate" form type, and review the most recent filing for the overall rate impact percentage. You can also use the OIR's CHOICES tool at choices.floir.gov to compare estimated premiums across carriers for your address and coverage profile.
